Is Social Media Enough, or Do You Still Need a Website?

2026-10-11

A growing number of small organisations run their entire online presence through social media. The shop is an Instagram grid, the enquiries come through direct messages, the updates go out on Facebook, and there is no website at all. It works, up to a point, and it is free and familiar — so the question naturally arises: in an age when everyone is on social platforms, does a business still need a website of its own? It is a fair question, and the answer is more nuanced than either the 'social is dead' or 'you must have a website' camps suggest.

This article is for owners and managers weighing exactly that decision: whether to rely on social media alone, invest in a website, or do both. The aim is to lay out the real trade-offs honestly, so you can decide based on your situation rather than on slogans.

What social media genuinely does well

It is worth being fair to social platforms, because for some organisations they are genuinely enough, at least for a while. They are free to start, people already use them daily, and they are built for reach and interaction — content can spread, audiences can engage, and you can reach people where they already spend their time. For a business that is informal, visual, early-stage, or community-driven, a lively social presence can carry a lot of the load, and insisting on a website before that makes sense would be premature.

So the honest starting point is that social media is not a poor substitute for a website in every case. For certain organisations, at certain stages, it really can be sufficient. The question is whether yours is one of them, and whether it will stay that way as you grow.

The catch: you are building on rented land

The fundamental limitation of relying solely on social media is that you do not own or control any of it. Your presence lives on someone else's platform, under their rules, subject to their changes. This is the issue that sits beneath all the others.

The platform decides who sees your content, and can change those rules overnight, so the reach you enjoy today is not guaranteed tomorrow. Your account can be restricted, suspended, or lost — sometimes through no fault of your own and with little recourse — taking your audience and your content with it. And you are bound by the platform's design and limitations: you present your business the way it allows, not the way you would choose. Building your entire presence on rented land means that if the landlord changes the terms, or evicts you, you have nowhere of your own to fall back on.

A website, by contrast, is something you own and control. It is your space, on your terms, that cannot be taken away by a platform's decision — which is precisely what social media cannot offer.

Where a website pulls ahead

Beyond ownership, a website does several things that social platforms struggle with, and these tend to matter more as an organisation grows and its needs become more serious.

Credibility and depth

For many people, a proper website is still a mark of a legitimate, established organisation in a way a social profile is not. It also gives you room to explain things properly — your services, your approach, detailed information — rather than in fragments scattered across posts that quickly scroll out of view. When someone is deciding whether to trust you with their money, that depth and permanence count.

Being found by people who are searching

Social media is largely about reaching people as they browse; a website is how you are found by people actively searching for what you offer. These are different and complementary kinds of discovery, and relying only on social means missing everyone who is looking for you rather than passively scrolling.

Control over the experience

On your own website you decide how visitors are guided, what they see first, and how they are led towards getting in touch or buying. On social media you are confined to the platform's layout and limitations. That control matters when you are trying to turn interest into actual enquiries and sales.

The sensible answer for most: both, in balance

For most organisations, the real answer is not website or social media but both, each doing what it does best. Social media is excellent for reach, engagement, and meeting people where they are; a website is your owned, credible home base that you control and that can be found by searchers. Used together, social draws people in and the website gives them somewhere solid to land and act.

Where exactly the balance falls depends on your situation — your sector, your audience, your stage, how you actually win work. A visual, impulse-driven business may lean more on social; a considered, trust-dependent service may need a stronger website. Working out the right mix for your specific circumstances, rather than following a one-size-fits-all rule, is where impartial advice helps, and a web consultancy can help you judge whether social alone is genuinely serving you or quietly limiting you, and what kind of website — if any — would actually move things forward.

Questions worth asking

A few questions help you judge whether social media alone is still enough for you:

1. If our main social account vanished tomorrow, would we still have a way to reach our audience? 2. Are we missing people who search for what we offer rather than scroll for it? 3. Does our business ask people to trust us in ways a social profile alone may not support? 4. Can we explain what we do properly on social, or only in fragments? 5. Are we on social by genuine choice, or just because it was free and easy?

That first question is the sharpest test. If losing one platform account would effectively erase your online presence, you are more exposed than you may realise — and that exposure is the strongest argument for having something of your own.

Own your home, use the high street

Social media and a website are not rivals; they are different tools for different jobs. The mistake is treating social platforms as a complete substitute for a presence of your own, when what they really offer is reach on rented land. For some organisations, at some stages, that is genuinely enough — but for most, the resilient approach is to use social media for what it does best while keeping a website you actually own and control as your home base. Think of social as the busy high street where you meet people, and your website as the premises you own and they can always come back to. Decide the balance deliberately, based on your situation, and you get the reach without the fragility of depending on a platform that was never really yours.
