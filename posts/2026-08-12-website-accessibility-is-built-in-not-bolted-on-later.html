Website Accessibility Is Built In, Not Bolted On Later

2026-08-12

Most organisations treat website accessibility as something to sort out later — a compliance box to tick once the 'real' work is done, or a specialist add-on to consider if a complaint ever arrives. That framing is the root of the problem. Accessibility is not a feature you attach to a finished website; it is a set of decisions baked into how the site is built, and trying to retrofit it afterwards is slower, more expensive, and rarely as good.

This piece is for the people who commission and manage websites rather than code them: the marketing lead, the operations manager, the business owner who wants their site to work for everyone but is not sure what that actually requires. The aim is to make accessibility concrete and practical, rather than a vague obligation you keep meaning to look into.

What accessibility actually means

At its simplest, an accessible website is one that people can use regardless of how they browse. That includes people using screen readers, those who navigate by keyboard rather than a mouse, people with low vision who need larger text or stronger contrast, and anyone with a temporary limitation — a broken arm, a bright screen in sunlight, a noisy environment where audio is useless.

It helps to drop the assumption that this is a small, separate group. Accessibility needs are broad, situational, and often temporary. Design for them and you improve the experience for everyone, because the same choices that help a screen-reader user — clear structure, readable text, sensible labelling — also help a rushed visitor on a phone with a patchy signal.

Why retrofitting is the expensive path

The reason accessibility belongs at the start is straightforward: the decisions that make a site accessible are structural, and structure is cheap to get right early and costly to change late.

Consider a few examples. Whether images carry meaningful text descriptions is decided when content is added, not months later when someone audits the site. Whether the page can be navigated by keyboard depends on how the interface is built. Whether colours meet contrast requirements is a choice made when the design is set. Each of these is nearly free to do correctly from the outset. Left until later, each becomes a separate remediation project, unpicking work that has already shipped.

This is why the 'we will make it accessible afterwards' plan so often stalls. Afterwards, it is not a tidy final step; it is a series of changes that reach back into design, content, and code all at once.

The building blocks worth knowing

You do not need to become an expert, but understanding the main components helps you ask the right questions and recognise whether they have been handled.

Structure and headings

A well-structured page uses proper headings in a logical order, so that someone using a screen reader can understand and navigate the content. This is the same structure that helps search engines and skim-readers, which is why good structure quietly serves several goals at once.

Text alternatives for images

Images that carry meaning need a text description so that people who cannot see them still receive the information. Purely decorative images should be marked as such, so they are skipped rather than announced. The distinction is simple once someone is in the habit of making it.

Keyboard navigation

Everything that works with a mouse should also work with a keyboard — links, buttons, menus, forms. A visible focus indicator, showing where you are on the page as you tab through it, is part of this. Sites that trap keyboard users, or hide where they are, exclude people for no good reason.

Colour and contrast

Text needs sufficient contrast against its background to be readable, and colour alone should never be the only way information is conveyed — an error shown only in red, with no text or icon, is invisible to some users. These are design decisions with a clear right answer.

Forms and labels

Forms are where many sites fall down. Every field needs a clear, properly associated label, and errors need to be explained in text rather than signalled by colour alone. Since forms are usually where enquiries and sales happen, getting this wrong has a direct commercial cost as well as an accessibility one.

A practical starting point

If you are not sure where your site stands, you do not need a full formal audit to begin. A few checks reveal a great deal.

1. Try navigating your own site using only the keyboard. Can you reach everything and see where you are? 2. Check whether your images have meaningful descriptions where they carry information. 3. Look at your text against its background — is it comfortably readable, or faint? 4. Fill in your own contact form and see whether the labels and any error messages are clear. 5. Ask someone unfamiliar with the site to complete a key task and watch where they struggle.

These informal checks will not catch everything, but they surface the most common and most damaging problems, and they cost nothing but a little time.

Building it in from the start

The organisations that handle accessibility well are rarely the ones with the biggest budgets. They are the ones who treated it as a normal part of building and maintaining a website, rather than a special project bolted on under pressure. When accessibility is part of the brief from the beginning — considered in the design, the content, and the build — it stops being a daunting, separate undertaking and becomes simply how the site is made.

For organisations planning a new site or a rebuild, this is the moment to raise it, because it is far easier to include than to add. Whether you work with an external partner or an in-house team, the principle is the same: raise accessibility at the start, not after launch.

Better for everyone, not just some

It is worth ending on the point that is easiest to miss. Accessibility is often framed as an obligation — something you do for a minority, because you should. But the choices it demands are simply the choices that make a website clearer, faster to understand, and easier to use for absolutely everyone. Readable text, logical structure, forms that explain themselves, and controls that work however you happen to be browsing are not concessions; they are the marks of a well-built site. Treat accessibility as part of quality rather than a box to tick, and you end up with a better website — which was always the point.
