How to Move a Website Without Losing What You Built

2026-08-19

A website move sounds like a technical formality — new platform, new host, maybe a fresh design, and everything carries across. In reality, a migration is one of the riskier things an organisation can do to its online presence, because so much of what makes a site valuable is invisible and easy to leave behind. Search visibility, working links, years of content, and the small technical details that keep everything connected can all vanish in a poorly planned move, and the damage often does not show up until weeks later when the enquiries quietly dry up.

This article is for the people responsible for that decision: the marketing lead, operations manager, or owner about to change platform, host, or domain. The aim is not to make you an engineer, but to help you understand where migrations go wrong and how to protect the things that matter before you press the button.

Why migrations are riskier than they look

The trouble with a website is that the version visitors see is only part of it. Beneath the pages sits a web of connections: the addresses search engines have indexed, the links other sites point at you, the redirects that guide old URLs to new ones, the settings that make email and forms work. A migration disturbs all of this at once, and any piece left behind can break silently.

The classic failure is a drop in search visibility. If the addresses of your pages change and nothing tells search engines where they have gone, the ranking you built over years can evaporate. The pages still exist; they have simply become strangers to the search engines that used to send you visitors. Because this unfolds gradually, it is easy to miss until the effect is well established and harder to reverse.

The assets you must not lose

Before any move, it helps to know exactly what you are trying to protect. Four things deserve particular attention.

Your URLs and redirects

Every page has an address, and other websites, search engines, and bookmarks rely on those addresses. If they change during a migration — which they often do — you need redirects that send each old address to its new equivalent. Skip this, and every existing link points at a dead end, taking both visitors and search value with it. Mapping old addresses to new ones is unglamorous work, and it is the single most important safeguard in most migrations.

Your content

It sounds obvious that content should come across, but pages are lost more often than you would expect — especially older ones that are still quietly useful. Before migrating, take stock of what you have, so you can confirm afterwards that nothing valuable was dropped. A page you forgot about may be one that a search engine still sends people to.

Your search visibility

Beyond redirects, the technical signals that help search engines understand your site — sitemaps, structured markup, page titles — need to survive the move intact. These are easy to overlook because they are invisible to visitors, yet they are part of how your site is found at all.

The connected services

A website rarely stands alone. Email, forms, analytics, and any integrated tools may depend on settings that a migration can disturb. It is entirely possible to move a site successfully and then discover a week later that the contact form has been quietly failing, or email has stopped arriving. Knowing what is connected lets you check each piece afterwards.

Planning a move that holds together

A good migration is mostly preparation. The switch itself should be the least eventful part, because the thinking was done beforehand.

Take a full inventory first

You cannot protect what you have not catalogued. Before anything moves, list your pages and their addresses, note the connected services, and record how the site currently performs so you have something to compare against later. This inventory is the reference you will check everything against once the new site is live.

Map old to new

If addresses are changing, build the map from every old URL to its new home, and prepare the redirects in advance. This is the work that preserves both your visitors' journeys and your hard-won search visibility, and it is far easier to do calmly beforehand than in a panic afterwards.

Test before you switch

Wherever possible, build and check the new site privately before it goes live, so problems are caught while they are cheap to fix. Once the switch happens, the clock is running and every fault is public.

Watch closely afterwards

The launch is not the finish line. For the weeks that follow, keep an eye on search visibility, broken links, form submissions, and enquiries, so that anything missed is caught early rather than months later. A migration is only truly finished when the new site is demonstrably doing everything the old one did.

Because the consequences of getting this wrong unfold slowly and can be expensive to undo, a migration is one of the moments where a second opinion earns its keep. Whether from an experienced in-house team or an outside web consultancy, having someone who has handled migrations before map the risks and check the redirects can be the difference between a smooth move and a quiet loss of visibility that takes months to notice and longer to recover.

Questions to ask before you migrate

Before committing to a move, work through five questions:

1. Are our page addresses changing, and if so, do we have a redirect for every one? 2. Have we catalogued our content so we can confirm nothing valuable is lost? 3. Will the technical signals that help us get found survive the move? 4. Which connected services — email, forms, analytics — might this disturb? 5. Who is watching the site in the weeks after launch, and what are they checking?

If you cannot answer the first two with confidence, you are not ready to migrate — you are ready to plan.

Move deliberately, not hopefully

There is nothing wrong with changing platform, host, or design; sometimes it is exactly the right decision. The mistake is treating a migration as a simple lift-and-shift when it is really a careful transplant, where the invisible connections matter as much as the visible pages. Plan for what you cannot see, protect the addresses and content you have built up, and watch closely once the new site is live. Do that, and a move becomes a genuine step forward rather than a quiet undoing of everything the old site had earned.
